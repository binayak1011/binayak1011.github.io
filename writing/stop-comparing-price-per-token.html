Stop comparing price per token

Three models score 46 on the same independent index. One costs 13 cents a task. Another costs $3.74. If you are choosing AI for real work, the per-token price is the least useful number on the page.

By Binayak Sarma23 September 20269 minute read
The short version
  • Per-token prices tell you what a word costs. Cost per task tells you what a finished job costs, and the two often rank models in opposite order.
  • At max effort, Opus 5.5 is 60 percent cheaper per token than GPT-6 Astra but 83 percent more expensive per task, because it writes about 4.4 times as many tokens.
  • The biggest lever on your bill is the effort setting, not the vendor. Start low, test on your own tasks, and move up only when they fail.

On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, three models score exactly 46. Xiaomi's open-weights MiMo-V2.6-Pro completes a typical index task for about 13 cents. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, at its lowest effort setting, costs 82 cents. SpaceXAI's Grok 4.7, at its highest, costs $3.74. Same score. Nearly 29 times the price.

None of those figures appears on a pricing page. They are cost per task: what it actually took to get a finished answer, once the model had read the input, thought for as long as it chose to, called its tools and written the result. That is the number that shows up on an invoice, and it regularly puts models in a different order from their sticker prices.

I use AI models in my own sourcing and reporting work, so this is not an abstract question for me. Everyone who runs AI in production learns the same lesson the first time a monthly bill arrives.

You were never really paying for tokens. You were paying for work.

The sticker price

Start with what the labs publish: the price per million tokens going in and coming out.

On the sticker, the story is simple. GPT-6 Luna is a hundred times cheaper than Astra and Fable 5.1. Claude Opus 5.5, released on 22 September at $4 in and $20 out, undercuts both of them by 60 percent. And on the same day, OpenAI launched GPT-6 Sol at exactly half of Opus 5.5's price.

If you stopped here, you would choose Opus 5.5 over Astra every time. Sometimes you would be wrong.

Why the sticker misleads

A model's bill is two numbers multiplied together: the price of a token and the number of tokens used. The labs compete loudly on the first and say very little about the second.

Reasoning models think before they answer, and that thinking is billed as output. Artificial Analysis counts it. At max effort, Opus 5.5 produces about 119,000 output tokens per index task. Astra produces about 27,000. Opus 5.5 charges 2.5 times less per output token, but it writes roughly 4.4 times as many.

2.5×lower price per output token for Opus 5.5 than for GPT-6 Astra
4.4×more output tokens per task from Opus 5.5, both at max effort

The measured result: at max effort, Opus 5.5 costs $5.98 per index task and Astra costs $3.26. The model that is 60 percent cheaper per token turns out to be 83 percent more expensive per task.

That is not the whole story, because Opus 5.5 also scores five points higher at that setting. Which raises the question no pricing page answers: how much capability do you get for each dollar?

The frontier

Plot every model, at every effort setting, by capability and by cost per task, and a clear pattern emerges.

The line marks the efficient frontier: the set of options where nothing else is both cheaper and more capable. Anything below and to the right of it is paying more for less. Five things stand out.

Opus 5.5 at high effort beats Astra at max on both axes. It scores 54 for $1.82 a task, against Astra's 53 for $3.26. This is why Artificial Analysis found four of Opus 5.5's five effort levels sitting on the frontier.

Astra held the frontier until 22 September. When it launched, Artificial Analysis found every one of its effort settings on the frontier. Opus 5.5 has since pushed all of them just off it. Astra at medium effort still scores 50 for $1.54, close to the line, and it remains by far the most token-efficient model at the top of the index.

The bottom-left corner belongs to OpenAI's small models. GPT-6 Luna at max effort scores 37 for 7 cents. At low effort it costs under half a cent per task, the cheapest result anywhere on the leaderboard.

Open weights have become a serious option. MiMo-V2.6-Pro scores 46 for 13 cents. The same score costs six times as much from Astra and more than 20 times as much from Grok 4.7.

Anthropic's own flagship now sits well off the frontier. Fable 5.1 at max effort costs $7.63 for a 53, a score Astra reaches for $3.26 and Opus 5.5 beats at high effort for $1.82. Fable remains Anthropic's Mythos-class model, with strengths this index does not fully capture, but for most everyday work its newer, smaller sibling has made it hard to justify.

The effort dial is the real price lever

Every frontier model now ships with an effort setting, from low to max. For your bill, it matters more than which model you choose.

The returns shrink quickly. Moving Opus 5.5 from high to max buys four index points and multiplies the cost per task by 3.3. Moving Astra from high to max buys two points for 1.9 times the cost. On Fable 5.1, going from xhigh to max adds nothing measurable to the score while adding 28 percent to the bill.

Max effort exists for the hardest problems, where a few extra points of capability are worth almost any price. For nearly everything else, it is a tax. The right default for most teams is the lowest setting that still passes their own quality bar, and finding it takes an afternoon.

Scale that to a month and the setting stops being a technical detail. Say a team runs a thousand tasks of index-level difficulty. On Opus 5.5 at max effort, that is about $5,980. At high effort it is $1,820, and at medium, the default in Anthropic's own apps, $1,340. Moving the default from max to high saves around $4,160 a month and gives up four points on the index. Moving from high to medium saves another $480 and gives up three more. Which trade is worth making depends entirely on the work, but it should be a decision someone makes deliberately, with a number in front of them, rather than a default nobody chose.

Vendor maths and independent maths

Claims about cost deserve the same scepticism as claims about capability. Anthropic says Opus 5.5 cuts the cost of typical workloads by about 40 percent compared with Opus 5. Artificial Analysis found it level with Opus 5 on cost per task at max effort, because the new model uses 1.6 times as many output tokens.

Both can be true. Anthropic's figure describes typical real-world usage across its products. The independent figure describes the most expensive setting on a fixed test. The lesson is not that either is wrong. It is that "cheaper" always needs two follow-up questions: at what effort, and on what workload?

Two smaller line items deserve attention, because they can quietly dominate a real bill.

Caching. When an agent rereads the same documents on every step, cached reads can become most of the cost. Opus 5.5 charges $0.20 per million cached tokens, a 95 percent discount on its input price. Fable 5.1 charges $0.25, a 97.5 percent discount. OpenAI discounts cached input by 90 percent, which puts Astra at $1.00. For context-heavy agent work, that gap compounds with every step.

Long prompts. Astra charges double for input, and one and a half times for output, on any request with more than 272,000 input tokens. If your workflow pushes whole contracts or data exports into a single prompt, that surcharge changes the maths.

Rework. Cheaper models carry hidden costs of their own. When Artificial Analysis tested GPT-6 Sol and Luna, it found both hallucinating far less than their predecessors, with Sol's hallucination rate falling from 92 percent to 60 percent on its knowledge test. Much of that improvement came from declining to answer more often: Sol attempted 83 percent of questions, down from 99. The same models also slipped on GDPval-AA, the professional-work benchmark, because their deliverables were shorter and more often left out required elements. A model that declines, or hands back an incomplete draft, costs you a retry or a person's time. Neither appears in the price per task.

Speed is the other invoice

Cost per task has a twin that never appears on a bill: time per task. On Artificial Analysis's measurements, Gemini 3.8 Flash at high effort produces nearly 300 tokens a second and returns a full response in about 16 seconds. Opus 5.5 at high effort takes around 18 seconds. Astra at max effort took more than five minutes. For batch jobs that run overnight, none of that matters. For anything a person is waiting on, a slightly less capable model that answers in seconds is often the better tool, because it keeps the human in the loop moving.

How I would choose, as an operations team

Put together, the data suggests a simple approach. Sort your work by how hard it is, not by which vendor you prefer, and match each tier to the cheapest option on the frontier that clears it.

To make that concrete with the kind of work I know: screening five hundred profiles against a fixed rubric is a tier one job. Drafting personalised outreach for the forty who pass is tier two. Reconciling three trackers into a pipeline report for a client call is tier three. Surprisingly little of an operations team's week belongs in tier four.

Three habits make this work in practice. Measure cost per completed task, not per token, and track it like any other unit cost. Start one tier lower than feels safe, and move up only when your own test set shows a real failure. And revisit the choice every quarter: this chart looked completely different in June, and it will look different again by the new year.

The labs are no longer competing only on who is smartest. They are competing on how much useful work you get per dollar, and that race is moving faster than the capability race. The single most useful thing an operations team can do is stop reading the sticker.
